Your Guide to Cracking the EPAM Online Assessment and Technical Interview
EPAM's on-campus process has 5 rounds, but 80% of rejections happen in just two of them: the Online Coding Assessment and the Java-heavy Technical Interview. This guide covers all 5 rounds briefly, but goes deep on exactly these two. You'll get a curated list of 35+ coding problems asked in real EPAM drives, the Java rapid-fire questions interviewers love, and mistakes that got real candidates rejected.


Senior Software Engineer at Atlassian

One thing before you start: EPAM generally allows candidates to code in Java, C++, or Python, although the exact options can vary across hiring drives. Since many technical interviews include Java and OOP discussions, preparing in Java is still a good choice if you're comfortable with it. If your strongest language is C++ or Python, you can typically use that for coding rounds, just verify the allowed languages for your specific drive.

OA Strategy
🎯 Since the assessment combines MCQs and coding questions, manage your time carefully. Finish the MCQs quickly without overthinking and reserve most of your time for the coding problems, since they usually carry higher weightage.

Test edge cases.

Spending 30–45 minutes attending the session can help you answer these questions confidently later.

Round 3: Group Discussion
~10 students, 1 moderator, ~30 min. Everyone typically gets one chance to speak, so make it count.

They're testing English fluency and structured thinking, not domain knowledge.

Don't relax: one drive forwarded the entire group; another eliminated a large chunk. It's unpredictable.

Real EPAM GD topics:

"What if Google Maps stopped existing?"

"Does family tradition limit your future?"

Use a simple 3-part structure when you speak: state your stance, give one concrete example, then a one-line conclusion. 45 seconds of clarity beats 3 minutes of rambling.

Round 4: Technical Interview
Format
60 to 90 min. Structure seen repeatedly: ~30 min Java/OOP rapid-fire, then 1-2 live coding questions, then project discussion.

Interviewers may be from a different domain (one candidate faced a Big Data panel for an SDE posting). Stay flexible.

Live coding may run on your own IDE, and you may be denied rough paper. Practice dry-running in the IDE itself.

Project Discussion
Expect: architecture of your project, why you chose X tech, problems you faced, how you handled obstacles.

Deployed, live projects (GitHub + hosted link) have repeatedly impressed EPAM interviewers.

If you have a Spring Boot + MongoDB project, be ready to defend your DB choice (one candidate defended MongoDB with a "flexible metadata" argument, and it worked).

Try to answer in STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) Format.

💡 If your project is just on localhost, deploy it this week. A live URL is the single cheapest credibility boost.

Round 5: Managerial & Final Interview
The final round is typically conducted by a manager or senior leadership representative. The discussion focuses less on coding and more on your communication skills, ownership, teamwork, career aspirations, and overall fit for the organization.


Explain one of your projects.
During my final year, I built an Online Food Ordering System using Spring Boot, React, and MySQL. The application allows users to browse restaurants, place orders, and track order status. I implemented user authentication using JWT, built REST APIs for order management, and integrated Razorpay for payments. One challenge was handling concurrent order updates. I solved it using optimistic locking to prevent conflicting updates. Through this project, I learned REST API design, database optimization, and how to structure a scalable backend.


What does EPAM mean? How did EPAM catch your attention?
EPAM stands for Effective People Advancing Management. I first came across EPAM through its strong reputation in software engineering and digital transformation. I also learned that EPAM invests heavily in employee learning through structured training programs, which really attracted me because I'm someone who enjoys continuously learning new technologies.


Tell me about yourself.
Hi, I'm Rahul Sharma, currently pursuing my B.Tech in Computer Science from XYZ University. I enjoy solving data structures and algorithms problems and have solved over 500 problems on LeetCode. Apart from DSA, I've built full-stack projects using Java, Spring Boot, React, and MySQL. Recently, I've been learning system design and cloud technologies. Outside academics, I enjoy playing badminton and mentoring juniors in programming.

If they ask about family:

I come from a supportive family of four members. My father runs a small business, my mother is a homemaker, and my younger sister is currently pursuing her graduation. My family has always encouraged me to focus on education and continuous learning.


Where do you see yourself in 5 years?
In the next five years, I want to become a strong software engineer who can independently design and build scalable systems. I also hope to mentor junior engineers and contribute to technically challenging projects. My primary goal is continuous learning rather than simply reaching a particular designation.


Tell me about a time you played a leadership role.
During my final-year project, our team initially struggled because tasks weren't clearly divided. I suggested creating a weekly plan and assigned responsibilities based on everyone's strengths. We also held short progress meetings every weekend. As a result, we completed the project two weeks before the deadline and successfully demonstrated all the planned features.


Tell me about a time you worked in a team.
During a hackathon, our team consisted of four members. I was responsible for the backend APIs while others handled the frontend and UI. We used GitHub for collaboration and held regular discussions to resolve integration issues. Even when requirements changed midway, we communicated effectively and completed the project on time.


How do you keep yourself updated with technology?
I regularly follow engineering blogs from companies like Google, Netflix, Uber, and Microsoft. I also watch conference talks on YouTube, solve coding problems regularly, and build side projects whenever I learn a new technology. This combination helps me understand both theory and practical implementation.



Note: Make sure you really have watched some and able to explain.


What are your strengths and weaknesses?
Strengths
One of my biggest strengths is that I enjoy solving problems and don't give up easily. If I don't understand something initially, I spend time researching and experimenting until I find the solution. I'm also comfortable learning new technologies quickly whenever required.

Weakness
Earlier, I used to spend too much time trying to make every solution perfect. Over time, I realized that delivering a good solution first and improving it iteratively is often more effective. I've been consciously working on balancing perfection with productivity.


How do you handle pressure or crises?
I try not to panic. First, I identify the highest-priority issue and break the problem into smaller tasks. Then I communicate with my team if required and work on resolving the critical issues first. I believe staying calm and communicating clearly is the best way to handle pressure.


What if your coworker is always late?
I would first speak to the colleague privately instead of raising the issue publicly. There could be a genuine reason. If the behavior continues despite the discussion and starts affecting the team's productivity, I would inform the team lead so that it can be handled professionally.


Why Java?
Java is the language I've spent the most time working with. It has helped me build a strong understanding of object-oriented programming, collections, multithreading, and backend development using Spring Boot. That's why I feel most productive using Java. However, I'm comfortable learning and working with other programming languages whenever a project requires it.


Do you have any questions for me?

What Happens After Selection (Good-to-Know)

The full process can stretch 2-3 months, so be patient between rounds.
